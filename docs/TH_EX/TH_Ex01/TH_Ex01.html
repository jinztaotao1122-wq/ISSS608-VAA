1 Overview

Visual analytics focus This submission investigates where motor insurance profitability weakens, where volatility concentrates, and which segments deserve underwriting attention.

1.1 Background

Insurers often know that a portfolio is underperforming before they understand why. This exercise uses a real motor insurance portfolio to show how visual analytics can move beyond dashboard reporting: the analysis combines distributional graphics, segment views and statistical validation to identify the portfolio segments most associated with weaker profitability and higher volatility.

The dataset was published on Mendeley Data as A detailed dataset of motor insurance policies with coverage-specific financial information. It contains anonymised motor insurance transactions from a Spanish non-life insurer between 2022 and 2024.

1.2 Analytical Questions

This report is organised around four questions.

  1. What is the shape of the portfolio across policy, driver, vehicle and geographic characteristics?
  2. How are premium, claim count, incurred loss and loss ratio distributed?
  3. Which segments are associated with weaker profitability, measured mainly by loss ratio?
  4. Which segments and coverages contribute most to claim frequency and volatility?

The analysis defines profitability as total_incurred / total_premium. Lower loss ratios are preferable because a smaller share of premium is absorbed by claims. Volatility is examined through claim concentration, loss-ratio dispersion and tail contribution.

6.3 Loss Ratio Dispersion

Code 
loss_sample |>
  mutate(
    loss_ratio_capped = pmin(loss_ratio, 3),
    policy_type_label = fct_reorder(policy_type_label, loss_ratio_capped, .fun = median)
  ) |>
  ggplot(aes(loss_ratio_capped, policy_type_label, fill = policy_type_label)) +
  ggdist::stat_halfeye(alpha = 0.78, adjust = 0.7, point_interval = median_qi, .width = c(.5, .8)) +
  scale_x_continuous(labels = percent) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = rep(insurance_palette, length.out = nlevels(loss_sample$policy_type_label))) +
  guides(fill = "none") +
  labs(
    title = "Sampled loss-ratio distributions by policy type",
    subtitle = "Loss ratios are capped at 300% for display; summary tests use the full uncapped data.",
    x = "Loss ratio",
    y = NULL
  )

The policy-type signal is statistically validated by a Kruskal-Wallis test of loss-ratio distributions: H = r round(unname(kruskal_policy$statistic), 1), p < 2.2e-16. A Fligner-Killeen variance test on log1p(loss_ratio) also rejects equal variance across policy types: chi-square = r round(unname(fligner_policy$statistic), 1), p < 2.2e-16. In short, policy type affects both profitability and volatility.

7 Statistical Validation

7.1 Frequency Model

To validate claim frequency drivers, a quasi-Poisson model was fitted with total_claims as the response and log(total_exposure) as an offset. Exponentiated coefficients are interpreted as claim frequency ratios relative to the reference category, holding other included variables constant.

Code 
freq_plot <- frequency_effects |>
  mutate(effect_strength = abs(log(estimate))) |>
  slice_max(effect_strength, n = 18) |>
  mutate(effect = fct_reorder(effect, estimate))

freq_plot |>
  ggplot(aes(estimate, effect, color = direction)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed", color = "#7c8581") +

    title = "Claim frequency ratios from quasi-Poisson model",

Comprehensive-no-excess policies have a much higher expected claim frequency than third-party-only policies, even after controlling for year, claims history, business origin, driver age, vehicle age, geography, payment frequency and fuel type.

8 Interpretation and Recommendations

8.1 Main Findings

The portfolio is profitable overall, but the average masks clear pockets of pressure. Loss ratio deteriorated in 2024, comprehensive-no-excess policies show the weakest profitability, and incurred loss is highly concentrated in a small tail of transactions. Liability and property damage are the biggest portfolio-level levers because they carry both high premium share and high loss ratio.

Claims history, payment frequency, business origin, vehicle/fuel characteristics and circulation area are useful secondary signals. They are not all causal, but together they help identify segments where pricing, underwriting rules or claims management should be reviewed.

8.2 Recommended Actions

  1. Review comprehensive-no-excess pricing and deductible design because it has both high claim frequency and high loss ratio.
  2. Monitor 2024 deterioration by separating exposure growth from severity inflation, especially in liability and property damage.
  3. Build a tail-risk monitoring view for large losses, because the top 1% of policies by incurred cost contributes almost half of total incurred loss.
  4. Use payment frequency and portfolio/new-business differences as underwriting diagnostics, then validate whether these are proxies for tenure, selection or customer risk.
  5. Treat vehicle and geography signals as candidate rating refinements, especially diesel, urban circulation and higher-value vehicle segments.

8.3 Limitations

The dataset is anonymised and does not include some common rating factors such as exact geography, claim dates, inflation indices, driving usage, no-claims discount details or external socioeconomic variables. The visual and statistical findings should therefore be interpreted as portfolio diagnostics rather than final pricing indications. A production actuarial model would require additional exposure validation, claim development checks and out-of-sample testing.

## Background

Insurers often know that a portfolio is underperforming before they understand why. This exercise uses a real motor insurance portfolio to show how visual analytics can move beyond dashboard reporting: the analysis combines distributional graphics, segment views and statistical validation to identify the portfolio segments most associated with weaker profitability and higher volatility.

The dataset was published on Mendeley Data as *A detailed dataset of motor insurance policies with coverage-specific financial information*. It contains anonymised motor insurance transactions from a Spanish non-life insurer between 2022 and 2024.

## Analytical Questions

This report is organised around four questions.

1. What is the shape of the portfolio across policy, driver, vehicle and geographic characteristics?
2. How are premium, claim count, incurred loss and loss ratio distributed?
3. Which segments are associated with weaker profitability, measured mainly by loss ratio?
4. Which segments and coverages contribute most to claim frequency and volatility?

The analysis defines profitability as `total_incurred / total_premium`. Lower loss ratios are preferable because a smaller share of premium is absorbed by claims. Volatility is examined through claim concentration, loss-ratio dispersion and tail contribution.

Younger drivers have high claim rates, but they are a small premium segment. Diesel vehicles show weaker profitability than gasoline vehicles, and urban circulation has higher claim frequency than rural circulation. These signals are useful for prioritising pricing refinements only after controlling for policy type and exposure.

# Interpretation and Recommendations

## Main Findings

The portfolio is profitable overall, but the average masks clear pockets of pressure. Loss ratio deteriorated in 2024, comprehensive-no-excess policies show the weakest profitability, and incurred loss is highly concentrated in a small tail of transactions. Liability and property damage are the biggest portfolio-level levers because they carry both high premium share and high loss ratio.

Claims history, payment frequency, business origin, vehicle/fuel characteristics and circulation area are useful secondary signals. They are not all causal, but together they help identify segments where pricing, underwriting rules or claims management should be reviewed.

## Recommended Actions

1. Review comprehensive-no-excess pricing and deductible design because it has both high claim frequency and high loss ratio.
2. Monitor 2024 deterioration by separating exposure growth from severity inflation, especially in liability and property damage.
3. Build a tail-risk monitoring view for large losses, because the top 1% of policies by incurred cost contributes almost half of total incurred loss.
4. Use payment frequency and portfolio/new-business differences as underwriting diagnostics, then validate whether these are proxies for tenure, selection or customer risk.
5. Treat vehicle and geography signals as candidate rating refinements, especially diesel, urban circulation and higher-value vehicle segments.

## Limitations

The dataset is anonymised and does not include some common rating factors such as exact geography, claim dates, inflation indices, driving usage, no-claims discount details or external socioeconomic variables. The visual and statistical findings should therefore be interpreted as portfolio diagnostics rather than final pricing indications. A production actuarial model would require additional exposure validation, claim development checks and out-of-sample testing.
